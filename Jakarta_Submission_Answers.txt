PROJECT NAME
Analog Atlas: Earth Test Sites for Moon and Mars Missions

SUMMARY (135 words; 926 characters)
Analog Atlas is a Python notebook and mapping workflow that identifies Earth locations worth investigating as rehearsal sites for Moon and Mars missions. Using the OSEMN framework, it compares Earth terrain with lunar south-pole and Martian Jezero reference patches, excludes listed analog sites, and checks shortlisted locations using satellite observations and published research. Our pilot sampled 384 Earth patches. Follow-up analysis identified Area A in Ha'il, Saudi Arabia, where hematite and montmorillonite clay spectral signatures recur on nine qualifying observation dates. The result is a candidate for investigating how rovers explore rocky terrain and select sampling locations. It is not yet a validated rover course or a confirmed new analog. By separating measured evidence from missing field tests, Analog Atlas helps teams decide where to investigate and what to measure before planning a mission rehearsal.

PROJECT DETAILS
WHAT WE DEVELOPED
Analog Atlas combines an OSEMN notebook, candidate maps, quality-screened satellite summaries and a documented field-test plan. Users can preview datasets with head(), change search settings, inspect evidence and export results. The public package includes the discovery notebook, the 16-granule Ha'il follow-up notebook and saved assessment summaries.

HOW IT WORKS
Obtain: download Copernicus Earth elevation and NASA LOLA/MOLA reference terrain. EMIT minerals and MODIS temperature/snow provide follow-up evidence.
Scrub: apply coverage and quality masks and exclude areas near a cited registry of known analogs. Missing measurements remain missing.
Explore: map 384 geographically stratified Earth patches and show dataset previews before analysis.
Model: compare slope, relief, roughness and low-slope fraction with a weighted distance score. A fixed seed makes sampling repeatable when inputs and settings are unchanged. This is a pilot search, not an exhaustive global search or a trained machine-learning model. Scores measure terrain resemblance, not probability of suitability. The known-site registry expanded after the original run, so a rerun with updated inputs can differ.
iNterpret: review published research, repeat satellite detections and the measurements still needed in the field.

WHAT WE FOUND
Area A is a 200 m inspection cell near 27.57670 N, 41.45848 E in Ha'il, Saudi Arabia. Hematite and montmorillonite spectral-library signatures recur on 9 of 9 qualifying dates. Across the broader study area, 14 of 16 EMIT files passed the scene screen, representing 11 dates. One excluded file had no pixel centres inside the boundary. The other had only 3.86% of pixels pass the clear-pixel masks, below the 10% minimum. These exclusions concern data coverage and quality, not whether Area A is suitable.
GLO-30 terrain gives a median slope of 4.7 degrees and a 90th-percentile slope of 8.8 degrees from 36 cell centres. This is context for reconnaissance, not a safe driving route. MODIS temperature and snow summaries describe the larger study patch, not this individual cell.

WHY IT MATTERS
The project connects a broad terrain search to repeat observations and explicit field-test questions. Its intended benefit is to help mission teams choose where to investigate before committing to a rover rehearsal. Operational savings have not yet been measured.

LIMITATIONS AND NEXT STEPS
Regional geology has been studied and exact-site novelty is unproven. Satellite mineral signatures require field spectroscopy and laboratory XRD. We must confirm access permission, survey rocks and soil at the rover's scale, then test wheel slip, localization and energy use. Snow screening does not establish extractable buried ice. Caves and construction suitability remain unverified. Earth cannot reproduce lunar or Martian gravity, atmosphere or radiation. No direct CRISM/M3 mineral comparison was completed.
